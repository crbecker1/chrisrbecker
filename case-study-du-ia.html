DU.edu: Information Architecture Case Study

How I led information architecture for the University of Denver’s website redesign at A Hundred Years Agency, using card sorting and information design theory to turn a ten-year-old sitemap into a navigation that helped lift applications by 18%.

The short version

The University of Denver had not updated its website in seven years, and its information architecture was more than ten years old. Working for A Hundred Years Agency, I led the IA across 12 weeks of discovery and design: we introduced information design theory to stakeholders, card sorted the existing site with about 53 people from marketing and admissions, and turned the results into a sitemap and navigation for desktop and mobile.

PProblem

A website that had outgrown its structure

Information is essential to any organization, and universities hold their sitemaps and page titles tightly. When we joined, DU.edu had not been updated in seven years, and its information architecture had not been revised in more than ten.

“The website is like a space, like a building. It represents the University to the outside world... our website needs to be the virtual space, the place, the information source, the image, the representation of everything wonderful that the University is, just like our physical campus does.”DU Steering Committee member

OOutcome

A navigation that is still in use

Our hypothesis was simple: by running a card sorting exercise, we believed the new information architecture would be truly informed by, and useful to, the people who use it.

Success meant a sitemap the university could lock in and use to restructure its content, and a navigation, for desktop and mobile, that was easy to find your way around. The new navigation structure became part of the final execution. It launched in 2018, and it is still in use at du.edu. Applications rose by 18%, and accessibility scores and SEO improved.

SStakeholders

Balancing a lot of voices

Working with university administrators, faculty representatives and the marketing department made this a tricky balancing act. The good news is that institutions of higher learning don’t shy away from the chance to learn, so we brought information design theory into the room to bring our clients up to speed and to explain the IA decisions.

Competitive analysis

To bring DU.edu into the modern web, we first looked at what the university space was doing.

Information architecture theory

We introduced information design theory to stakeholders so we could justify IA decisions. The ideas behind it are information foraging and information scent.

Four information scent best practices guided the work:

  • Be explicit. Labels should describe what users will find at their destination.
  • Show where you are. Give feedback about the current location.
  • Skip the jargon. Don’t use made-up words, institutional jargon or your own slogans as navigation items.
  • Use plain language. It helps SEO, usability and accessibility.

AArtifacts

What we made

Card sorting and analysis

Working with about 18 people from marketing and 35 from admissions, plus faculty representatives and a small set of students, we used Optimal Sort to card sort the current IA, then tested it and formulated a new path forward. We analyzed the results with a similarity matrix and dendrogram visualizations to show how the groups formed.

Sitemap and navigation strategy

An IA is not a sitemap, but it leads to one and, ultimately, a user interface. We grouped the card sorting results, tested them against hierarchy models, and drew a sitemap.

Final navigation UI

The sitemap became navigation for both desktop and mobile, designed as a mobile-first, responsive system.

LLessons Learned

What worked, and what I took from it

The team recognized early that the organization could only move so fast on decisions. That led to very different outcomes than we first expected.

What I took from it

IA and navigation are never finished. They are improved over time. A website is a living entity, and our solution gave DU room to add new ideas while keeping a solid foundation for users.

Navigation is negotiated. It has to be flexible enough to serve both users’ needs and the institution’s needs.

Let data inform the IA, not dominate it. Card sorting gave us a starting point, and judgment shaped the rest.

Information architecture is not a luxury for big projects. DU discussed and weighed the navigation updates for longer than expected because changing it had such deep implications for the institution. Usability, user-centered language and clear logic were what we wanted to infuse into the solution, and working with large groups of stakeholders means being willing to push and pull ideas.

What I’d do differently. I would have tested the navigation update earlier, with working prototypes. I would also have let the university team co-create more, since they took longer to come around to owning the changes.

Where it landed

We worked very hard to reach a final solution, and it is not etched in stone. That was the point.

The new navigation structure launched in 2018 and is still in use on du.edu. It brought an 18% increase in applications, better accessibility scores and SEO gains, and it gave the University of Denver enough room to keep improving it.
